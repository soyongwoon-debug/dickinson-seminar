# Presentation Script — "Trying to Think with Emily Dickinson"

> Full spoken script for an 18-slide, ~25-minute graduate seminar presentation
> on Jed Deppman, "Trying to Think with Emily Dickinson" (2005).
> Stance: the thesis is self-undermining — his own reading proves thought and feeling are inseparable.
> Delivery pace assumed: ~130 words/minute. Total ≈ 3,250 words ≈ 25 minutes.

---

## Slide 1 — Title  (~0:45)

Good afternoon, everyone. Today I'll be presenting Jed Deppman's 2005 essay, "Trying to Think with Emily Dickinson," published in *The Emily Dickinson Journal*. The title already tells us a great deal. Notice that Deppman does not say "thinking with Dickinson" — he says *trying* to think. That word, "trying," is the hinge of the entire argument, and by the end of this presentation I want us to feel the full weight of it. My goal today is twofold: first, to reconstruct Deppman's argument as clearly and fairly as I can; and second, to put pressure on it — to argue that the essay quietly undermines its own central claim, and that this is actually what makes it worth reading. So let's begin with the question the essay is really fighting about.

---

## Slide 2 — Contents  (~0:50)

Here's the road map for the next twenty-five minutes. I'll start with the critical debate Deppman enters — is Dickinson a mystic of feeling, or a serious thinker? Then I'll lay out his thesis and the statistical evidence he marshals for it. From there we move into his theoretical framework, which draws on postmodern theory and, crucially, on Kant's idea of the sublime. Next I'll trace what he calls the origin of Dickinson's "trying," and the evidence he finds in her letters to Higginson. That leads to his central concept — the "try-to-think" poem — which I'll then demonstrate through an extended close reading of one poem, "I tried to think a lonelier Thing." Finally, I'll give my critical assessment, stake out my own position, and open the floor for discussion. So there's a lot of ground, but it all builds toward that one poem and that one closing argument.

---

## Slide 3 — Two Epigraphs: The Central Tension  (~1:20)

Deppman opens with two epigraphs, and I want to dwell on them because they frame everything that follows. The first is from Dickinson herself, writing to her brother Austin in 1851: "Why not an 'eleventh hour' in the life of the *mind* as well as such an one in the life of the *soul*?" The "eleventh hour" is a religious image — the last chance for salvation. Dickinson is transferring that drama of salvation from the *soul* to the *mind*. In other words, for her, thinking is not a cool, detached activity; it carries the same existential stakes as the fate of one's soul.

The second epigraph is from Kierkegaard's *Sickness unto Death*: "the more consciousness, the more intense the despair." This is the darker side of the same coin. If thinking is an existential drama, it is not necessarily a happy one — greater consciousness can mean greater suffering.

So already, before the argument even begins, Deppman has planted a tension. Thinking matters enormously to Dickinson — but thinking hurts. Hold on to that tension, because I'm going to argue at the end that Deppman never fully resolves it, and that his inability to resolve it is the most honest thing about the essay.

---

## Slide 4 — The Critical Debate  (~1:30)

Now, why does Deppman need to make this argument at all? Because he's pushing against a well-established way of reading Dickinson. The received view treats Dickinson as a poet of *feeling* — of ecstasy, awe, and physical sensation. Remember her famous line to Higginson: she knows something is poetry when she feels physically as if the top of her head were taken off. Readers have naturally concluded that her power is emotional, even bodily, and that it does not depend on clarity of thought.

The most pointed version of this comes from Margaret Peterson, whom Deppman quotes: Dickinson's most impassioned poems, she says, become "a series of ecstatic assertions, an abandonment to excess verging on mental unbalance." That's a strong claim — it's essentially saying that at her most intense, Dickinson stops thinking and simply erupts.

Deppman's entry point is to take that opacity — the notorious difficulty of the poems — and reinterpret it. Where others see emotional excess or breakdown, he wants to see disciplined intellectual work. And so he announces that he will "argue the opposite case." Let's look at exactly what that opposite case is.

---

## Slide 5 — The Thesis  (~1:20)

Here is the thesis in Deppman's own words: he will "argue the opposite case and portray her not as a mystic but as a serious thinker." Notice the two contrasts he sets up. Against "ecstatic assertions," he offers "careful sequences of ideas and images." Against "abandonment to excess," he offers "thoughtful production of, and reaction to, extreme states of being."

So the claim is not just that Dickinson has ideas — every poet has ideas. The claim is stronger: that the *movement of thinking itself* is the real subject and the real achievement of her poems. His stated goal is to show how Dickinson conceived of the activity of thinking, how she linked thought and poetry, and — this is the phrase I'd underline — "how writing poetry helped her think."

I want to flag something right now that we'll return to. Deppman frames this as thought *versus* feeling — he's choosing thought. Keep asking yourself, as we go: can you actually separate the two? Because I don't think he can, and I don't think his own evidence lets him.

---

## Slide 6 — Evidence 1: Word Statistics  (~1:30)

Deppman's first move is admirably concrete. Rather than just asserting that Dickinson cares about thought, he counts. Using the Rosenbaum Concordance, based on the 1955 Johnson edition, he tallies her vocabulary. On the feeling side: "feel" appears 39 times, "felt" 35, "feels" 16, "feeling" 8; "body" only 10 times, "bodies" once. On the thinking side, the numbers are dramatically larger: "thought" 69, "think" 43, and "know" — this is the striking one — 230 times. "Know" is effectively the fourth most common verb in her entire corpus, after "be," "be able," and "have." "Mind" appears 79 times, "brain" 26.

The contrast is genuinely hard to ignore, and Deppman concludes that her poetry is "much more about thought than feeling." I think this is the strongest, most falsifiable part of the essay — it's a claim you could actually go and check.

But — and this matters for my later argument — Deppman himself immediately qualifies it. He admits the statistics are "partial and decontextualized." And he's right: counting how often a word appears tells you what a poet talks *about*; it does not tell you her *attitude* toward it, and it certainly doesn't prove that thought is somehow opposed to, or separable from, feeling. So even his best evidence has a crack in it that he himself points out.

---

## Slide 7 — Framework 1: Postmodern Dickinson  (~1:30)

Having established that thought pervades the poems, Deppman asks where Dickinson's ideas about thinking come from, and he places her on a map of critical theory. He draws on several critics. Cynthia Griffin Wolff calls her an "artist of the age of transition," whose labor was to free language "from the tyranny of His definitions" — that is, from God's authority. David Porter describes the Dickinsonian mind as "explosive with signifying power but disinherited from transcendent knowledge." In other words: enormous intellectual energy, but no stable God to anchor it.

Deppman then reaches for postmodern theory. He invokes Lyotard's famous definition of the postmodern as "incredulity toward metanarratives" — and argues that Dickinson, who neither fully accepts nor fully rejects the grand explanatory systems of her time, embodies exactly that skepticism. He also borrows Derrida's term "bricoleuse," casting Dickinson as someone who mixes and extends religious, literary, and scientific vocabularies, cobbling together meaning from available fragments.

The root of all this, Deppman says, is a specific cultural condition: the tension between the Lockean empiricism that saturated her schoolbooks and the Kantian, Transcendentalist idea that the mind can apprehend the supersensible. Dickinson stands in the gap between those two systems — and it's the Kantian side that becomes the engine of the whole essay.

---

## Slide 8 — Framework 2: The Kantian Sublime  (~1:40)

So let's look at that engine carefully, because everything downstream depends on it. Deppman borrows the structure of the Kantian sublime — the very scene that Lyotard turns into his account of the postmodern condition.

Here's how it works, in four steps. First, reason conceives of something that is *conceivable but unpresentable* — infinity, death, the infinitely large or small. Second, reason demands an image adequate to that idea. Third, the imagination tries to supply the image — and fails; the thing is simply too big to picture. Fourth, the mind keeps trying anyway, and out of this repeated straining, this try-and-fail loop, the *feeling* of the sublime is born. Lyotard reformulates this as the task of the artist: "to invent allusions to the conceivable which cannot be presented."

Now, I want you to notice the word "feeling" in step four, because it's going to matter. Even in Deppman's own theoretical frame, the sublime is fundamentally an *experience* — an intense inner state. The thinking and the feeling arrive together.

And here is the concession we have to be honest about: Deppman admits that Dickinson "did not read Kant." His claim is not one of influence but of a *shared attitude* — a structural analogy. That's a legitimate move, but it's a vulnerable one, and I'll come back to it in my critique. For now, hold the question: if she never read Kant, is the sublime something Deppman *finds* in the poems, or something he *brings* to them?

---

## Slide 9 — The Origin of "Trying" (L10, 1846)  (~1:30)

Deppman locates the origin of Dickinson's lifelong project of difficult thinking in a letter she wrote at just fifteen, to her friend Abiah Root. In it, she tries to think her own death, and she cannot. Listen to the language: "I cannot imagine with the farthest stretch of my imagination my own death scene... I cannot realize that the grave will be my last home."

What Deppman notices is the *rhythm* of failure. The letter repeats: "I cannot imagine... I cannot realize... I cannot realize... nor can I realize." He describes the mind here as one that "stretches, fails, realizes it fails, regroups, rewords, and reaches its limit again." That's the Kantian loop from the previous slide, playing out in real time in a teenager's letter.

And this gives us the core formula of the whole essay — the definition of "trying to think." Dickinson, Deppman says, "cannot think death or Eternity... but she cannot not think them either." That double bind — *cannot* and *cannot not* — is the engine of the try-to-think poem. She is compelled toward the unthinkable and unable to leave it alone. Notice, though, that even here the compulsion is emotional: it's *dread* of death that keeps her returning. The trying is driven by feeling.

---

## Slide 10 — Evidence 3: The Higginson Letters  (~1:30)

Deppman's next body of evidence is Dickinson's correspondence with Thomas Wentworth Higginson — the only sustained professional literary exchange of her life. His argument is that Dickinson consistently understood *writing as thinking*.

Look at the famous opening question from 1862: "Are you too deeply occupied to say if my Verse is alive?" She's not asking whether it's pretty or correct — she's asking whether the *thought* in it is alive. And she explains why she can't judge this herself in a wonderful line: "The Mind is so near itself — it cannot see, distinctly — and I have none to ask." That's an epistemological problem: the mind cannot get far enough away from itself to see itself clearly.

But — and this is the detail I want to seize on — notice *why* she says she writes. Not for beauty, not for fame. She writes: "I had a terror... I could tell to none — and so I sing, as the Boy does by the Burying Ground — because I am afraid." Deppman himself glosses this: for Dickinson, writing is "thought's psychotherapeutic response to troubling emotion." I'll say that again — *psychotherapeutic response to troubling emotion*. That's Deppman's own phrase. So even as he builds his case for thought, he tells us that the thinking exists to manage *feeling*. And she carried this to the end: in her last months she wrote that illness had left her "bereft of Book and Thought."

---

## Slide 11 — Defining the "Try-to-Think" Poem  (~1:10)

All of this converges on Deppman's central and, I think, genuinely valuable contribution: the concept of the "try-to-think" poem. The purpose of such a poem is to force the mind to do something extremely difficult — to satisfy reason's unsatisfiable demand for a complete image of the unpresentable.

He gives the defining features: the try is serious, the goal is explicitly stated, and the emphasis falls squarely on "the willful movements of thought." These poems, he says, are "precisely sequenced, if difficult, thought experiments" — and, importantly, they invite the reader to repeat the steps, to try the thought along with her. Examples include "Of Death I try to think like this," "The nearest Dream recedes," and "I think To Live — may be a Bliss."

This is a real critical achievement, because it names a genuine, recurring pattern in Dickinson and gives us a tool we can carry to other poems. So let's now watch it operate on the poem Deppman treats as the masterpiece of the type.

---

## Slide 12 — Close Reading 1: Fr570 and the Dilemma  (~1:40)

The poem is "I tried to think a lonelier Thing," from 1863. Let me read the frame of it. It opens: "I tried to think a lonelier Thing / Than any I had seen — / Some Polar Expiation — An Omen in the Bone / Of Death's tremendous nearness." It moves through probing, borrowing, plucking, and ends: "I almost strove to clasp his Hand, / Such Luxury — it grew — / That as Myself — could pity Him — / Perhaps he — pitied me."

Deppman poses a sharp interpretive dilemma about this poem. Is the "trying" here *proactive* — a virtuoso attempt to conceptualize an extreme human possibility, thinking as intellectual sport? Or is it *reactive* — an attempt, in his words, "to knead it, battle it, alter it, realize it, or just survive it through thought"?

And here is a decisive moment for my argument: Deppman chooses *reactive*. He explicitly says the poem's purpose is not to invent an extreme experience but to *deal with one that has already arrived*. Think about what that concession means. If the poem is reactive, then the pain — the loneliness — comes *first*, and the thinking is the response to it. The feeling is the cause; the thought is the coping. That's not thought instead of feeling. That's thought *because of* feeling. I'll return to this.

---

## Slide 13 — Close Reading 2: Tracking the Steps  (~1:50)

Let's honor Deppman's method and actually track the sequence of thought, because this is where his reading is most impressive. The poem proceeds in stages.

First, the speaker tries to *name* the lonely "Thing," and produces two attempts — Deppman calls them "quiddities." The first is "Some Polar Expiation." Expiation is a religious purging of the self; but "Polar" puns on polar *expedition*, so the phrase fuses moral self-punishment with the image of being stranded at the frozen ends of the earth — radical exile from self, culture, and nature. The second is "An Omen in the Bone / Of Death's tremendous nearness" — no longer looking backward but forward, a felt premonition of death near in both time and space.

Second, the speaker reaches for company: "My Duplicate — to borrow." She imagines another creature, also "Of Heavenly Love — forgot" — someone else abandoned by God. Deppman calls this "the smallest possible unit of imagined community." She is so alone that she has to *invent* a fellow sufferer.

Third comes the crucial ambiguity — the phrase "Within the Clutch of Thought." Deppman shows it is undecidable: does the duplicate exist somewhere reachable *by* thought, or is it made *only of* thought — purely fabricated? Everything hangs on this. Either she has found real company in misery, or she is desperately manufacturing her own consolation out of nothing.

And fourth: "I plucked at our Partition." Note the possessive — "our," not "the." That tiny word forms a *we*: the partition between the living speaker and her imagined dead twin becomes both a barrier and a bridge. The mind has generated a relationship where there was only solitude.

---

## Slide 14 — Close Reading 3: The Paradoxical Close  (~1:30)

Now the ending, which is genuinely strange and moving. The speaker and her invented duplicate are two God-forsaken souls in "Opposing Cells" — Deppman compares it to a scene from Beckett's *Godot*, two isolated figures who can almost, but not quite, reach each other. And then: "I almost strove to clasp his Hand, / Such Luxury — it grew — / That as Myself — could pity Him — / Perhaps he — pitied me."

Deppman does something brilliant here. He hears an *elided phrase* in the gap — he reads it as "Such Luxury — it grew [to think] — / That as Myself — could pity Him." In other words, the luxury, the strange comfort, comes from the *experimental force of thought itself*. The mind has thought its way to a fragile companionship, and that is the "luxury."

But listen to how Deppman describes the achievement: it is "a fragile state and a momentary victory." How long, he asks, can meditating on one's own dead duplicate actually console you? And here's my point: the victory is fragile precisely because it is *emotional*. A logical proof doesn't decay; a feeling of comfort does. The instability of the ending is the instability of a *mood*. So the poem that supposedly demonstrates the triumph of thought actually ends by showing thought's dependence on a passing emotional state.

---

## Slide 15 — Critical Assessment: Strengths  (~1:15)

Before I push back, let me give the essay its due, because it earns it. I see four real strengths.

First, methodological rigor. Deppman grounds a big interpretive claim in concordance data — that's a falsifiable, checkable move, and it's rare and admirable in literary criticism. Second, genuine integration: he uses the letters not as decorative biography but as actual evidence of a poetics — the letters and the poems illuminate each other. Third, and most durable, the "try-to-think poem" is a generative concept. It names a real pattern, and any of us could take it and apply it to poems Deppman never discusses. Fourth, he reframes Dickinson's famous difficulty. Where earlier critics saw opacity as failure or as emotional excess, Deppman lets us see it as the visible *trace of a mind at work*. That's a genuine gift to how we read her.

So this is a strong, serious essay. Which is exactly why it's worth pressing on its central claim.

---

## Slide 16 — Critical Assessment: My Position  (~1:50)

Here is my position, stated plainly: **the essay's thesis is self-undermining — and that is the most interesting thing about it.**

Deppman sets up thought *versus* feeling and chooses thought. But watch the language of his own close reading. He calls the poem "desperate." He calls the final scene "chilling." He speaks of "sickening loneliness." And, as we saw, he defines Dickinson's writing as "thought's psychotherapeutic response to troubling emotion." Every one of those is an *affective* term. His reading doesn't run on cool cognition — it runs on dread, on desperation, on the need for comfort.

So here's the contradiction. If the "try" in these poems matters *only because* the loneliness is unbearable — and Deppman himself insists the poems are reactive, dealing with pain that has already arrived — then thought and feeling cannot be pulled apart. The trying *is* an emotional act. The binary he builds his essay on collapses under the weight of his own evidence.

But I don't think this makes the essay a failure. I think it makes it *truer than its thesis*. What Deppman actually demonstrates — against what he sets out to prove — is that in Dickinson, thinking and feeling are the same gesture: to think the unthinkable *is* to suffer it, and to suffer it *is* to be driven to think it. That's the real discovery here, and it's a better one than the thesis he announced.

I'll add three quicker pressure points for discussion. One: "Kant without Kant" — since Dickinson never read Kant, is the sublime a lens Deppman *finds* or one he *imposes*? Two: there's a whiff of circularity — the "try-to-think poem" is defined *from* the very poems it's then used to explain. And three: Deppman rejects Paul Muldoon's reading of this poem as a Civil War allegory — a rejection worth questioning, and a good place for us to disagree.

---

## Slide 17 — Conclusion and Discussion Questions  (~1:15)

Let me draw it together. Deppman ends on an honest and unsettling note: we never actually know whether Dickinson *succeeded* in thinking what she tried to think. What we know is that these are poems in which she "tried to help or save herself by representing her own efforts to help or save herself." And to read them, he says, is to risk "experiencing a loneliness we cannot sound."

Notice how that returns us to our opening epigraph from Kierkegaard — the more consciousness, the more despair. Thought, in Dickinson, is never pure consolation. It is consolation *and* risk at once. Which is exactly why I've argued that you cannot cleanly separate her thinking from her feeling — and why Deppman's essay is most valuable precisely where it fails to keep them apart.

Let me leave us with four questions for discussion. First: does the thought/feeling binary survive Deppman's own close reading — and should it? Second: is the Kantian sublime a valid frame for a poet who never read Kant? Third: is the "try-to-think" poem a genre Deppman *discovered*, or one he *constructed*? And fourth: whose reading of "I tried to think a lonelier Thing" do you find more convincing — Deppman's, or Muldoon's Civil War reading? Thank you — I'd love to hear your thoughts.
